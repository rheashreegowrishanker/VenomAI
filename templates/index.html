What is VenomAI?

VenomAI is a research tool that reads the amino-acid sequence of a venom peptide and estimates which kind of molecular target it most likely acts on, and whether it is likely to be toxic.

It was trained on curated records from ArachnoServer (spiders), ConoServer (cone snails) and UniProt, covering spider, scorpion and cone snail venoms.

Target prediction

Ranks the most likely target classes, such as ion channels, receptors, enzymes and membrane-associated targets.

Toxicity check

Shown only when the toxicity model passes our reliability checks.

Explanations

Every result links to the sequence features that pushed the model towards its answer.

Why does it matter?

Venoms are libraries of thousands of peptides shaped by evolution to hit specific targets. Some became medicines, such as ziconotide, a painkiller derived from a cone snail peptide. Others inspire research tools and pest control.

Testing each peptide in the lab is slow and expensive. A computational first pass helps researchers decide which peptides to look at first.

A bare number isn't enough for a scientist. VenomAI shows why it answered the way it did, so the result can be questioned, not just trusted.

How to use it

  1. Paste a sequence. Standard amino-acid letters only, 5 to 200 long.
  2. Choose the animal group. This doesn't change the prediction. It tells you how far to trust it.
  3. Read the result, then open "Why did the model say this?" to see what drove it.

Always confirm important results against UniProt or ArachnoServer before relying on them.

What it can and can't do

It can

  • Rank the top three likely target classes with a confidence
  • Show which sequence features drove a prediction
  • Give a useful first pass for scorpion peptides

It can't

  • Replace laboratory experiments
  • Give a reliable toxicity verdict, because our toxicity model did not pass its checks
  • Cover snakes, bees or other venom sources

How well did it do in our tests?

Score = balanced accuracy when the model is tested on one animal group at a time. Scores shift with how the data is split, so treat them as approximate.

Lower scores reflect fewer examples per target class and labels read from database text, so these groups are flagged as a data limitation rather than a finished result.

Toxicity: not reliable. In our checks the model did not beat a simple length-and-cysteine baseline, and fell below our reliability threshold on a matched test.

Target labels come from database text annotations, not direct experimental labelling.
